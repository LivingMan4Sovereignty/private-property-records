Part II — Rights vs. Privileges & Licensing

1. Liberty Is Inherent; Privilege Is Granted

In my understanding, there exists a fundamental distinction between an inherent right and a government-granted privilege.

A right belongs to a living man or woman by virtue of existence and is not created by the issuance of a government document.

A privilege, by contrast, is a particular benefit, authorization, or permission granted under conditions established by an institution or governing authority.

I believe this distinction deserves careful consideration whenever governmental regulation intersects with the ordinary liberties of human life.

My freedom to move from place to place, provide for my family, visit loved ones, pursue meaningful work, and transport my personal property is, in my conscience, part of the natural liberty bestowed upon me by my Creator.

I do not regard that underlying liberty as a commodity owned by government or something that first comes into existence when an administrative office issues its approval.

I recognize that government establishes requirements governing particular activities, including the operation of motor vehicles upon public roadways.

Nevertheless, I maintain that the existence of such requirements does not make government the original source of my fundamental liberty.

Government may regulate activities within its lawful authority, but it does not thereby become the creator of the inherent rights of mankind.

I believe that those entrusted with public authority should continually examine whether regulations serve their legitimate purposes while respecting the constitutional protections and individual liberties of the people.

The distinction between a right and a privilege should never be dismissed merely because administrative systems have become familiar or longstanding.

Government must not presume that an inherent liberty becomes its property merely because it has established an administrative process concerning the exercise of that liberty.

I recognize that different constitutional rights receive different forms of legal protection and that courts have upheld motor-vehicle licensing and registration requirements.

My concern is not resolved, however, merely by observing that a regulatory system presently exists or has been upheld in particular circumstances.

I believe the people retain the responsibility to question whether governmental restrictions are necessary, proportionate, constitutionally justified, and administered with respect for individual liberty.

When an individual is traveling peacefully, presenting no apparent danger, and causing no injury to another living soul or their property, I ask those entrusted with enforcement authority to consider the underlying purpose of the regulation and whether punitive action is necessary.

Where lawful discretion exists, I believe respectful communication, education, and restraint should be considered before imposing financial penalties or compelling an individual to defend peaceful conduct through court proceedings.

I do not believe that the mere existence of an administrative requirement should end the discussion concerning liberty, constitutional limitations, and the proper exercise of governmental power.

My position is that government exists to serve and protect the people, and its authority must remain accountable to the constitutional framework under which it operates.

The right of individuals to travel upon public highways and transport their property has long been discussed in American judicial decisions.

I find particular significance in historical decisions recognizing that ordinary travel is closely connected to liberty, the pursuit of happiness, and the everyday affairs of life.

In Thompson v. Smith, 155 Va. 367, 154 S.E. 579 (1930), the Supreme Court of Appeals of Virginia addressed the revocation of a permit to operate a private automobile.

The court recognized the right of individuals to travel public highways and transport property using the ordinary conveyances of their time.

Importantly, the court also held that the exercise of this right remained subject to reasonable public-safety regulation, while rejecting arbitrary restrictions and unequal treatment.

I regard this decision as a meaningful recognition that ordinary private travel is not merely a discretionary favor granted by an administrative official.

Likewise, in Chicago Motor Coach Co. v. City of Chicago, 337 Ill. 200, 169 N.E. 22 (1929), the Supreme Court of Illinois distinguished ordinary highway travel associated with personal or business affairs from the extraordinary commercial use of public streets as a transportation business.

The decision recognized a distinction between ordinary public use and the operation of commercial passenger transportation for private gain.

I find that distinction important because I ordinarily travel for personal, family, and household purposes rather than operating a transportation service for compensation.

These historical authorities reinforce my conviction that ordinary movement is closely associated with individual liberty and should not be treated as a privilege that officials may arbitrarily withhold.

In Hendrick v. Maryland, 235 U.S. 610 (1915), the United States Supreme Court upheld reasonable motor-vehicle registration and licensing requirements as exercises of state authority concerning public safety and highway use.

I acknowledge that holding while maintaining my conviction that every exercise of governmental power should remain accountable to constitutional limitations and the principles of fairness and due process.

The recognition of governmental authority to regulate does not mean that such authority is unlimited or beyond constitutional examination.

I believe the historical recognition of ordinary travel as a liberty deserves continuing consideration whenever officials exercise discretion concerning peaceful travelers.

My request is that public servants consider not only the existence of an administrative requirement but also the underlying principles of liberty, public safety, proportionality, and equal treatment.

I maintain that the purpose of constitutional government is to protect the people and their liberties, not to presume unlimited authority over the ordinary affairs of life.
